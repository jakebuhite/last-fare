# Monetization Plan

**Recommendation:** sell it as a premium game at a low price, with no microtransactions. Add paid content later if it sells. Treat the numbers below as starting assumptions to check against comparable games, not as forecasts.

## Why premium and not microtransactions
- Small, single-player horror games sell on a one-time price. Players of this genre actively distrust in-app purchases.
- Our design (dread, scarcity, knowledge as progression) is damaged by paid shortcuts or "buy a safer car" mechanics. The pillars in `GAME_DESIGN.md` conflict with pay-to-win.
- A paid game on Steam needs no live service, store backend or payment compliance work beyond the platform's.

## Pricing
- **Launch price: $9.99–$14.99**, depending on content length at launch. A ~2-3 hour polished slice with replayable nights fits $9.99. Moving toward 5+ hours of content supports $14.99.
- **Launch discount of 10-15%** for the first week to catch wishlisters.
- Regional pricing on Steam defaults are fine; review them at launch.
- Later: join seasonal sales at 25-50% off, and bundle with DLC.

## Revenue paths, in order of effort
1. **Base game sales (primary).** Steam first (PC), then itch.io. Consoles later only if PC performs.
2. **Free demo** containing the first fare (The Quiet One). This is the single most useful marketing asset: it supports Steam Next Fest, streamer coverage and wishlist conversion.
3. **Paid DLC / expansion nights** (e.g. new passengers and a new story chapter, $3.99-$4.99). Fits our data-driven passenger design: cheap to produce once the systems exist.
4. **Optional cosmetic or soundtrack DLC** (soundtrack, art book). Low revenue but good will; never gameplay-affecting.
5. **Not planned:** loot boxes, energy timers, ads, paid shortcuts. If we ever add cosmetics (car air fresheners, paint), they stay cosmetic and small.

## Platform economics (verify current terms before relying on these)
- Steam keeps about 30% of revenue; there is a one-time app fee (about $100) that is recoupable after early sales.
- Add tax and refund effects (Steam refunds within 2 hours of play). A short, strong first impression matters more than usual.
- Rough planning math: revenue to us per copy at $12.99 is about $9 before tax and any publisher/studio cut. At 10,000 copies that's about $90,000. Treat wishlist count as the lead indicator; many indie teams plan around wishlist-to-sale conversion in the range of 10-20%, but this varies widely.

## Marketing hooks that match the product
- **"You are the Uber driver"** is instantly explainable. Name the genre in the store tagline; avoid trademarked app names.
- **Streamer-friendly**: jump scares in a repeatable loop, and dashcam clip sharing (M09) is built for clips.
- **Daily seeded shift** (see `REPLAYABILITY.md`) gives streamers and communities a reason to return after launch.
- Build the Steam page and start collecting wishlists as soon as there is a trailer-worthy fare, long before launch.

## Milestones tied to money
| When | What | Why |
|---|---|---|
| After vertical slice | Steam page, trailer, wishlist push | Wishlists drive launch visibility |
| ~3 months before launch | Public demo, Next Fest | Largest single visibility event for small games |
| Launch | $9.99-$14.99, launch discount, press/streamer keys | |
| Post-launch | Patches, daily shift, then paid DLC nights | Keeps the long tail alive |

## Open questions
- Platforms beyond PC? (Console ports cost real time; decide after launch data.)
- Do we want a publisher, or self-publish? Self-publishing keeps ~all post-platform revenue but needs the marketing work above.
- Business setup: a legal entity, tax forms and a payout account are needed to release on Steam. Worth sorting out early.
